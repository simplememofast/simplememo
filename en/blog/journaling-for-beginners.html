Everyday reflection

Journaling for Beginners

Journaling is a practice of recording events, thoughts and feelings. This guide uses it for everyday reflection: separate what happened from your interpretation and, if useful, choose a small next action. Choose a manageable format rather than a required length or an unbroken daily streak.

By AI ATAKA · Published: March 6, 2026

Start with a line for what happened, how you interpreted it, and what you might do next. Leave any unwanted field blank. The worksheet below works on paper or in your usual notes tool.

Updated September 9, 2026. The author develops Simple Memo. The example and worksheet are original practical suggestions from this site, not a psychological test, treatment program or evidence of health benefits from an app.

This fictional example illustrates the format; it is not a user testimonial. Use it to separate what you know from what you assume, without presuming that every problem is your fault.

Five steps for your first entry

  1. Choose a place you can revisit, such as paper, a local note or your usual editor. Check who can access it.
  2. Pick one manageable event. You do not need to revisit a painful experience; an ordinary observation is enough.
  3. Separate the event from your interpretation. Bullet points or individual words are enough; polished prose is unnecessary.
  4. If useful, choose one small next action. You can leave the question open and stop without resolving it.
  5. Decide whether and when to revisit it. A weekend review is one example; reduce the length or frequency if it feels burdensome.

In the morning you might note what to pay attention to; in the evening you might reflect on events. This guide prescribes no optimal time or duration. You may record something you appreciate, but you do not have to manufacture gratitude or a positive conclusion.

Distinguish research writing tasks from everyday reflection

Baikie and Wilhelm’s 2005 review covers expressive-writing studies with defined conditions. Findings vary by participants and outcomes, and short-term distress can increase. These findings do not establish the same health effects for everyday notes or a particular app.

Baikie & Wilhelm (2005): review of expressive-writing research

If writing makes you feel worse, you can stop or switch topics. If distress persists and affects daily life, support from a suitable service or professional is an option; writing does not have to carry the burden alone.

Check access on paper, on a device and in email

An email to yourself still passes through a sending service and a mailbox. It does not guarantee that only you can read it. Check device locks, shared accounts, notification previews, sync destinations and backups, and keep material you do not want to transmit out of email.

Simple Memo is one option for sending short text to a configured destination. Encryption of data stored on a device is different from end-to-end encryption across the whole delivery path. Choose a tool and destination that fit the content.

Frequently asked questions

What is journaling?
It is a practice of recording events, thoughts and feelings. This guide uses it for everyday reflection, separating events from interpretations and optionally choosing a small next action.
What should I write?
Choose one manageable event and record what happened and how you interpreted it. Feelings and next actions are optional. You do not need to choose a painful experience or fill every field.
How long and how often should I write?
This guide sets no required duration or daily schedule. Start with one line and adjust for effort and ease of review. You do not have to backfill missed days.
Will writing always make me feel better?
There is no guarantee. Research expressive-writing tasks and everyday notes differ in their conditions. Stop or change topics if writing worsens distress, and consider support if difficulties persist.
Should I use paper or an app?
Choose something easy to revisit with access you understand. Paper, a local note or your usual editor is sufficient. If sending email, also consider the delivery path and access to the mailbox.
Can I use the worksheet for free?
The Japanese and English worksheets can be copied or downloaded as Markdown without an account. Use them on paper or in another app; Simple Memo is optional.
