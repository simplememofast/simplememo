Glossary — Time Management

What is Timeboxing?

Timeboxing assigns a fixed time block to each task, requiring completion or a checkpoint within that window. It fights Parkinson's Law — "work expands to fill the time available" — using deadline pressure to dramatically boost focus and productivity. Famously practiced by Elon Musk among others.

By AI ATAKA

Definition

Timeboxing is a time management method that assigns fixed durations to tasks. Unlike a to-do list that specifies only "what," timeboxing also defines "when" and "how long." When time's up, you move to the next box regardless of completion. This constraint generates focus and prevents perfectionism and procrastination.

How to Practice Timeboxing

1. Assign Time Blocks
Assign 30 min, 1 hour, or 2 hour fixed blocks to each task. Place them as "blocks" on your calendar.
2. Focus with a Timer
Set a timer at the start of each box. Focus exclusively on the target task. No email, no social media.
3. Move On When Time's Up
When the timer rings, move to the next box regardless of completion. Schedule a new box for unfinished work.
4. Review
Review each timebox's output at day's end. Improve estimation accuracy and apply learnings to tomorrow's schedule.

Why It Works — Parkinson's Law and a Visible End

Parkinson's law is the observation that "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion" (C. N. Parkinson, 1955). Fixing the time first forces you to decide up front what "good enough" means, which stops perfectionism from inflating the task.

The second effect is easier starts. Much procrastination comes from not seeing an end. "Just 30 minutes" guarantees one, which lowers the barrier to begin. Scrum's sprints and daily scrums are run as fixed timeboxes for the same reason.

Timeboxing vs Time Blocking vs Pomodoro

The three are often confused, but each fixes something different: timeboxing fixes the maximum time you may spend, time blocking fixes calendar slots, and Pomodoro fixes the rhythm of focus and breaks. They combine well — set timeboxes inside a calendar block and run Pomodoros inside the box.

How Long Should a Timebox Be?

You are deciding how long you are allowed to spend, not how long it will take. When in doubt, go shorter and book a second box if needed. Four rough tiers:

15 min — clear it
Email replies, daily reports, quick lookups. A box for clearing rather than finishing; one at the start and one at the end of the day keeps the inbox from piling up.
30–45 min — the workhorse
A first draft, a review, one bug fix. Long enough to get somewhere, short enough to stay focused. The default when unsure.
60–90 min — the ceiling for deep work
Planning documents, design, writing — deep work. Close to the limit of sustained focus, so keep boxes of this length to two or three a day with short boxes or breaks between them.
Buffer boxes — insurance for the plan
Leave one unassigned 15-minute box in the morning and one in the afternoon. Overruns and interruptions land there, leaving the other boxes intact. A day with buffer left over is evidence your estimates are improving.

Why It Stops Working, and What to Do

You keep running over
Overrunning is not forbidden — it means booking a new box. Leave a one-line note on why the time ran out and next week's estimates improve. A task that overruns three times has a decomposition problem, not a length problem.
Interruptions break the box
Requests that interrupt you, and ideas that pop up mid-task, get written down rather than handled — then you return to the box. Writing them down must take seconds, or the box itself erodes; keep a capture tool within reach.
Too many boxes, planning becomes a burden
Keep it to three to five boxes a day; the rest is free time or buffer. A plan that boxes every hour collapses at the first interruption and gets abandoned. Only the work that tends to expand needs a box.

A Sample Day

Six boxes, only one of them deep work

9:00–9:15 Email (15 min)
9:15–10:00 Proposal outline (45 min)
10:00–10:15 Buffer (overruns and interruptions)
10:15–11:45 Proposal body (90 min)
13:00–13:30 Review replies (30 min)
13:30–14:00 Weekly review (30 min)

Send a one-line memo at the start of each box ("outline in 45 min") and at the end ("45 min: three outline options"), and the gap between estimate and actual records itself — next week's review is already done.

FAQ: Timeboxing

What is Timeboxing?

A time management method that assigns fixed blocks to tasks. Fights Parkinson's Law by using deadline pressure to boost productivity.

How is it different from Pomodoro?

Pomodoro uses fixed 25+5 cycles. Timeboxing sets flexible durations per task. Pomodoro = rhythm; Timeboxing = scheduling.

How do I log timeboxes with Simple Memo?

Send declaration and review memos at the start/end of each timebox. At 5 seconds per memo, your email becomes an automatic work log.

How is timeboxing different from time blocking?

Time blocking places slots on your calendar; timeboxing sets a maximum time you may spend on each task. They combine well: set timeboxes inside a calendar block.

What if I don't finish within the box?

Stop rather than extend, and book the remainder as a new box. A one-line note on why the time ran out improves your estimates. If the same task overruns three times, split the task differently.

How long should a timebox be?

Shorter when in doubt. 15 minutes for email, 30–45 for a draft, and no more than 90 for deep work such as design or writing — with at most two or three boxes of that length per day.

Are Scrum sprints a form of timeboxing?

Yes. Sprints, daily scrums, sprint reviews and retrospectives are all run as timeboxes with a fixed maximum duration.
